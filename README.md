## About This Research

This project examines cyber harassment and stalking in the Maldives, particularly focusing on what's still missing from the law even after the December 2025 Penal Code amendments. While Maldives recently updated its cybercrime legislation to align with the Budapest Convention, there's a critical gap - harassment and stalking aren't actually addressed in the new laws.

The main question I'm exploring is: How does this legislative absence affect vulnerable groups in Maldives, especially journalists and women in politics?

## What I Found

The situation is pretty concerning. In 2024 alone, there were 173 reported cybercrime cases, which is a 74.7% increase from the previous year. The impact on vulnerable groups is severe - about a quarter of female politicians have shut down their social media accounts because of harassment, and 60% of female journalists report experiencing online violence. Financial crimes are also a huge problem, with $2.2 million lost to scams in 2022. Despite all this, as of December 2025, there are still no specific laws addressing cyber harassment or stalking.

## The Dataset

I've compiled three main datasets for this research:

The primary dataset contains 30 documented harassment cases from 2019 to 2025. These cases came from news reports, human rights organizations, and parliamentary inquiries. The types of harassment range from death threats and doxxing to physical assaults and coordinated smear campaigns.

The secondary dataset includes official statistics from the Maldives Police Service, the Westminster Foundation for Democracy study, Bank of Maldives financial crime data, and Media Bill voting records. This helps provide context and validate the trends seen in individual cases.

The legislative comparison dataset looks at cyber laws across six South Asian countries, making it easier to see where Maldives stands compared to its neighbors and what gaps exist in its legal framework.

## Visualizations

I created several charts using Python to help visualize the data. These include a breakdown of different harassment types, a pie chart showing who's being targeted, a timeline of cases from 2019 to 2025, analysis of which platforms are used for harassment, police statistics comparing years, and a regional comparison of existing laws.

## How I Did This

For the primary research, I manually collected publicly documented harassment cases from international media outlets, human rights organizations, parliamentary inquiries, and police announcements. The secondary research involved compiling official statistics from multiple sources including the Maldives Police Service, Bank of Maldives, Westminster Foundation, and the International Federation of Journalists.

The analysis included quantitative work looking at harassment types, targets, and trends over time. I also did comparative legislative analysis with other countries in the region and temporal analysis that shows a clear escalation in 2024-2025.

Throughout this research, I made sure to follow ethical guidelines - only using information that's already public, not including personal details beyond what's already in news reports, taking a victim-centered approach that avoids sensationalization, and complying with UWE Bristol's research ethics requirements.

## What Needs to Happen

Based on what I found, here's what I think Maldives needs to do:

First, there needs to be immediate legislative action to create specific cyber harassment and stalking provisions in the law. The current amendments just don't cover this. Second, platforms need to be held accountable - there should be local mechanisms for content moderation that understand the Maldivian context. Third, victims need support services specifically designed for harassment cases. Fourth, there should be nationwide digital literacy campaigns to help people understand and protect themselves from these threats. Finally, Maldives could learn a lot from neighboring countries, particularly Sri Lanka's Online Safety Act from 2024.
